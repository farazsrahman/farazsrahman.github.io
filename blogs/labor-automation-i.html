Automation and Labor Markets I: The Real Alignment Problem

Written by: Faraz Rahman

Toiling away in the press, Sigismund Gelenius was “an extraordinarily learned man, and worthy of far better things” (Grafton). After study in Pavia, Bologna, and Venice, then teaching in Prague, Gelenius found himself working alongside the hand-laborers of the Froben Press for most of his adult life. Amidst a technological revolution deemed one of the most important for human progress, was there not a better place for Gelenius to take his talents? Since Gelenius, the first industrial revolution and the dawn of computing have transformed labor markets repeatedly and raised the questions about human labor including

  • How do new technologies impact how people spend their working hours?
  • What features of markets amplify or alter these impacts?

AI has revived this discussion, but we now have 500 years of human history since Gutenberg’s press to better understand how markets move under the influence of automation. I am writing this blog to better understand how labor markets will reconstitute in the coming years (2026-2030). Technology gives rise to impacts on varying time-scales - the printing press enabled the proliferation of an educated society, and the industrial revolution closed the loop between science and technology. It is fair to say that these second-order effects dominate the long-run impact of a technology, but they are rarely felt in the days, months, or years after an invention. This blog will focus on the shorter time-horizon - i.e. the first-order effects of technologies and what (I think) we can learn from history to navigate the 3-5 years ahead

Prosperity and Peril for the Handweaver

In 1812, an English textile manufacturer received the following:


“You will take notice that if [the machines] are not taken down by the end of next week I shall attach one of my Lieutenants with at least 300 men to destroy them [...] – the General of the Army of Redressers, Ned Ludd, Clerk".

The Luddites organized a movement with tactical precision (Liversidge). Their efforts earned them a place in most history textbooks, but the term luddite has become a diminutive for someone ignorant of or opposed to technology. It’s easy to forget that their factory rebellions were a natural response to the shifting tides of economic power at the turn of the 19th century.

Textiles were a dominant industry in the late 1700s. At its peak ___ workers were involved and customers were in ___. There were two parts to the industry. The first, spinning, was the process of turning raw materials (e.g. cotton, wool, flax) into yarn, and the second, weaving, processed the yarn into textiles for consumer purchase throughout the British empire. Both were done through what we would now call a gig-economy where merchants would contract families to spin or weave at home. Beyond a source of income the cottage industry was a way of life that gave families agency over their work and time with each other.

As Thompson put it[^1]:


“Weaving had offered an employment to the whole family [...] The young children winding bobbins, older children [...] helping to throw the shuttle in the broad-loom; adolescents working a second or third loom; the wife taking a turn at weaving in and among her domestic employments. The family was together, and however poor meals were, at least they could sit down at chosen times.”

Tied to the global British mercantile system, markets fluctuated considerably, and a shortage of labor in the 1760s inspired inventors such as James Hargreaves and Richard Arkwright to productionize their spinning jenny and waterframe technologies. Their efforts automated much of the spinning process and caused the entire market for hand-spinning to collapse (Allen, 2017). Spinners were out of the market, but an abundance of cheap factory yarn made hand loom weaving incredibly profitable for common families during the 1780s to 1810s. Further, entrance into the hand weaving market was an easy escape hatch to the displaced spinners often had participated in both markets anyways. The automation inadvertently led to the “golden age of hand loom weaving”.[^2] (Acemoglu, 2024; Allen, 2017). [find quote about how good life was]

A reckoning

Seeing the success of the mechanization in spinning, Edmund Cartwright set out to accelerate weaving production almost as soon as the golden era began. In 1785, he patented what is now credited as the first power-loom, but it took a few decades and successors, William Radcliffe, Thomas Johnson, William Horrocks, Richard Roberts and more to finally land on a recipe that could operate without human attendance. Their efforts culminated in a wave of scaling that led to 5,732 looms in Manchester and the surrounding areas in 1821 which is estimated to have doubled in two years by 1823 (Guest, 1823).


[Presumably Horrock’s Power Loom though I should confirm the source – much more intricate than the jenny.]

The rapid expansion was catastrophic for real wages quantitatively. The typical handweaver in 1820 could purchase less than half the quantity of food they could twenty years earlier. The market crash was bad for two reasons in particular. First, unlike spinning, there was no downstream business for working-people to capitalize on the increased weaving productivity. All productivity growth went to factory owners and downstream consumers, many of whom were outside of England and saw mass availability of cheap textiles products (CITE). For the weaver, changing sectors was capital intensive, requiring a family to relocate to the region of England needed to participate. Second, for those who took up factory work, a living wage was provided in exchange for… living.

Women and children (employed at lower wages than men) worked 12 hour shifts day and night to keep the looms running. The work was dreary and dangerous. Children were not given an education and the government provided no support to protect them, until the 1850s. The factory system had consequences on the mental and physical well-being of the English working class. As wage-takers the laboring class now lacked both economic and political power, so violence and destruction were one of a few options to voice the demands to those who had lost their way of living.

The fall of the weaver tells an important story. The jenny and power-loom were sibling technologies, but they lead to very different outcomes for the economic pie. What drove the difference was not technology, but rather the underlying market they were deployed in. I think this is a point worth emphasizing since modern discourse usually centers conversation when it is their relationship that matters the most. That being said two natural questions are:

  1. What indicators to understand when technology in a market will lead to growth or harm?
  2. How have technology and markets aligned in other technological eras?

For the first question, average productivity is what an engineer might use to measure whether what they are building works or not. But for economic growth it can be deceptive. Factory weavers, with machines, could produce up to 20 times the amount of a hand-weaver, but metric doesn’t acknowledge that factory weavers captured little-upside, and that many workers could not even access the job. Recent Nobel Prize winner, Daron Acemoglu advocates for marginal productivity – the increase in profits for an additional worker – as a more honest measure (Acemoglu, 2024). Factory owners saw no additional income from new labor if current machines already operated at peak volume placing laborers at potentially zero marginal-value. On the other hand, falling costs for threads due to automation increased the marginal profitability of every additional laborer just 30 years earlier.

For the second question, let’s move a century forward.

The Engine Roaring ‘20s

Through the late 1800s more than half of Americans worked on agricultural land, but the tractor, McCormick reaper, and soon-to-be developed Haber-Bosch process promised agricultural productivity without need for human labor. By the 1910s, it seemed that Americans could face the same plight of their English brethren a century earlier. The market did shrink, by the 1930s only 21% of Americans worked on farms, but the economic developments that followed were very different (FIND CITATION).


“The Chief Business of the American People is Business” - Calvin Coolidge

Hollywood celebrities, retail purchases and leisure activities powered by credit cards and consumerism captured the zeitgeist of the 1920s. Stocked with conservatives, Calvin Coolidge’s administration pushed laissez faire economics at a point when American businesses were ready to boom. Refrigerators, passenger flights, and more were powered by developments in chemistry, electricity and manufacturing. Troves of new jobs opened up as rural workers migrated to metropolitan hubs. Above all, the automobile was the icon for American life in the 1900s, and most economic productivity was a result or a reflection of the industry behind them.

[Image of Assembly Line]

So what made the difference? Most factors boil down to a simple principle: automation in the 1900s created markets where there were more things to do, and importantly these jobs had positive marginal productivity which balanced the declining value of human labor in agriculture.

By the end of the 1920s, Henry Ford’s assembly line dropped the price of an automobile to $260, less than a tenth of what it had been previously (Henry Ford Museum). At the time, most Americans did not have cars, so demand skyrocketed for automobiles as Ford built plants in ___, ___, and ___. At its peak, 200,000 cars were being produced per year. Ford employed ___ blue-collar workers, but more than this demand for engineers, repair mechanics, highway construction, up-stream materials processing, and down-stream salesman also grew, opening up roles across America and helping create what we now call “white-collar” work.

Within Ford’s factories, profits-driven decisions drove upward mobility and living standards. Mechanics trained in factories learned skills to innovate and the number of patents grew from 2,193 to 67,370. For some, patents paved a path to become entrepreneurs. For those who did not make riches, the nature of automotive work sat at a sweet-spot where it was accessible to unskilled job-seekers but challenging enough that a trained employee was a valued asset. When ___ wrote to Henry Ford of the dangerous conditions her husband faced in the factory, Henry Ford responded with a $5 a day wage doubling the previous, established the Henry Ford Hospital, and started providing benefits such as pension plans. Ford’s decisions were not out of kindness: worker turnover and factory strikes damaged revenue, so maintaining high morale helped Ford make more and better cars.

What qualities of the markets can we identify as helping make the 1900s a positive reinforcing loop:

  • Strong american purchasing power (financing) → elastic demand market → immediate response to automation enabling more work
  • Strong complementary (domestic) markets + laissez faire pro-(small)-business economics→ easy to start/grow a business and create jobs in {dealerships, repair shops, parts shops, etc.} → employees have stronger purchasing power (first point)

The Real Alignment Problem

Language Models are pretty good at what they do now. (Experiments studying their productivity impacts in medical, legal, consulting, programming, and call center work have shown consistent gains in quality and completion speed).

However, there is no point that was made as unequivocally as this: language models disproportionately benefit less-experienced workers. [add some numbers here]. On one hand, this seems self-evident. The vibe-coding zeitgeist is built on the fact that novice programmers can build in a few prompts what used to take a senior engineer days. On the other hand, the job market seems to have bent in the opposite direction. Entry-level white-collar roles have been a “bloodbath” for new college grads and junior employees have faced consistent fear of layoffs.

Once again, thinking about the market helps untangle this technological paradox. Automation can only make a firm more productive when the amount of work they do grows as well. If work is constant, a 2x speedup in getting tasks done means that only half the firm is necessary to provide outputs on the same timeline. If senior members also produce the highest quality output, then the marginal productivity of a junior member isn’t just lower, it’s negative and layoffs will follow. Many SV leaders have tried to calm automation angst claiming the technology will only augment productivity, but in a world with finite or slowly growing amounts of work, this will still move Americans out of jobs. Economists have called this phenomenon in general the productivity J-Curve (Brynjolfsson).

America’s economy is diverse and different industries will have different responses to automation. Conversation around “AI alignment” is dominated by technologists hoping for AI to reflect human values, but aiming automation at businesses ready for growth poses the more pressing problem of aligning AI with economic value. I am a fan of Yijia Shao’s work which surveys AI researchers and workers across industries to better understand what tasks “can be” automated and what “should be” automated at present. Automation desirability is only a proxy for marginal productivity, but their responses often indicated desire for automation was driven because it freed up time for “high-value” work (i.e. increasing MP).

The most insightful plot I found scattered Y Combinator startups and AI agent research papers along the axes of automation capability and desirability.

Overall, between the four zones the distribution of startups is uniform (this is not necessarily an indictment on YC, the distribution without them could be skewed in a bad direction; all surveys are imperfect oracles). I don’t want to sound prescriptive, a distribution skewed further up on the desirability axis would align with PG’s famous line to “make something people want”. Regardless of what they think the optimal spread is, the visualization seems useful to make better informed decisions as an investor or new-founder.

Further, I’m interested to see how the performance of companies in each zone play-out over time. In particular, I wonder whether the “Red Light” or R&D zone will turn more profits for the incubator. My optimistic hope would be that R&D zone wins since it aligns with Paul Graham’s famous line to “make something people want” [don’t use this twice].

Duality

Framing labor and automation as an alignment problem reminds us that there are separate levers to pull on either side: technology can be aimed at the current market, but markets can also be prepared for oncoming automation. Returning to 1900s America, auto productivity was driven by elastic demand markets, strong complementarities, laissez-faire economics, and a willingness for Americans to uproot their lives to take on new opportunities.

We cannot copy and paste market conditions, but the framing might be useful to think about things the government can do. The MIT Work of the Future article is relevant here.

Some ideas they discuss include:

  • People need to be flexible, and educational / vocational systems should help this. As Americans adapt, they may find themselves in between jobs. So long as they are working towards new employment, their purchasing power should be protected.

  • New businesses will need to be created, regulation should make this easier for the small / medium-player + purchasing power of the American should be protected so that growth is not choked.

  • Tax codes should incentivize protecting workers instead of machinery

  • Increased funding towards basic research not focused on by the private sector is a beneficial way to redirect STEM talent

The MIT team derived these ideas from first principles but they incidentally all push us closer to the market conditions from the 1900s. Migration from rural America meant that most were open to very different jobs than what they were used to, additionally the nature of jobs themselves didn’t require significant experience so the labor supply was adaptable to new industries as they appeared. New industries did appear and quickly in part thanks to Coolidge’s low-interest and tax-rates. [do some more research on the Progressive movement after the 2nd IR in America and how that affected labor / employer power]. Most engineering disciplines are complimentary markets to automation in software.

Conclusion

Markets are complicated and AI is a polarizing issue. Strong takes on either end of the spectrum – AI moratoriums or blind-growth – seem suboptimal. Unlike the 19th century weavers, we have tools to monitor and understand the situation. Jobs displacement will happen in the upcoming years, it is already happening, but aiming technology at applications ready for growth might help.

Predictions

I don’t work directly on developing / investing in the LLM application-layer, so predictions are a concrete way to post-hoc judge whether my thinking is right.

  1. STEM talent will be redirected from software to physical sciences:
  • Penn’s M&T program takes ~50 of the most talented engineering students each year. 2025 saw a sharp decline in those studying CS, and MechE was the most subscribed major. I see this as a market response to the publicized layoffs and automation happening in the Valley.
  • New talent will fuel development in other science and engineering disciplines. In 10 years, AI will compete for attention with another field that benefitted from the labor in-flow.

  1. The consulting market will collapse: the work of consultants is accelerated by AI, but AI enables companies to do more of the work they might otherwise hire a consultancy to do. If demand is cut by ½ and labor needed for a unit of work is cut in ½ then the overall labor requirement decreases by ½ ✕ ½ = ¼.
